There is a particular kind of Londoner who treats the support act like the adverts before a film. Doors at seven, headliner at nine, so you're in the pub round the corner until 8:45, sinking a pint you've timed to the minute, then sprinting up the road with your ticket open on a phone at 4% battery.

Totally get it. I am often that man. But I've come round to the view that this is the single biggest act of self-sabotage in British gig-going.

The support slot is football's academy system, the reserves playing on a Tuesday night in front of thirty people and two dogs. It's where nearly every band you love served its apprenticeship: playing to half-empty rooms, the bar queue louder than the vocals, a sound engineer who'd clearly rather be at home. The headliner is the product. The support act is the pipeline. The issue is that if nobody turns up to watch the pipeline, eventually there's no product.

The scene survives on support acts. Here's the band that taught me that, and two December gigs worth getting to early for.

## The Brògeal story: a meet-cute for the ages

We weren't planning on getting there for the support act, and I knew nothing of them going into it. It was 2023, the Kentish Town Forum, and we were there for The Mary Wallopers. Five young lads walked on looking like they'd just rolled down from playing their college prom. Their 40-minute slot was complete imperfection, but it was one of those shows where you get the feeling.

The 'Falkirk-ness' was unavoidable. My southern, English sensibilities were shown the other end of the British spectrum. I felt like Mary Berry taking a studs-up tackle from Scott Brown, or a chorister getting flashed by a drunk man in a kilt.

It was thrilling.

The song that broke the camel's back wasn't even an original. After 35 blistering minutes they finished (as they always do) with their will-one-day-be-fabled rendition of the Fureys' late-60s folk tune 'The Lonesome Boatman'. It's made up of short flurries of shrill whistling that slowly build towards an absolute tornado of a crescendo as more and more instruments join the procession. You can't help but stomp your feet.

It has an almost Spaghetti Western feel. You feel like a snake being charmed: lured into finding the nearest unmanned horse, saddling up and pillaging the local Aldi of whatever poor, unassuming Tennent's cans it has left in stock.

Listen to the Fureys' original and it's hard to fathom the chaos Brògeal inject into what started life as a pretty, mildly rousing folk song. If Eddie, Finbar, Paul or George Furey had been told that over half a century later there'd be scrums of screen-addicted millennials moshing to 'The Lonesome Boatman', they'd have put their Taytos down and had another sip.

### Who are Brògeal?

Five friends from Falkirk, the Scottish industrial town best known for that marvel of barge engineering, the Falkirk Wheel. It sits at the top of a rough triangle with Glasgow to the south-west and Edinburgh to the south-east. As a window-shopping Englishman, these young men strike me as decidedly more Glasgow than Edinburgh.

Their gigs are an hour of stout-sponsored chaos. Lead singers Daniel Harkins & Aidan O’Collaghan are flanked by acoustic and electric guitars, drums, accordion and bouzouki making up the orchestra. And it does _feel_ like an orchestra. There's a lot going on. I've watched them death-stare more than one sound engineer floundering to keep up.

Sunglasses have always struck me as a handy litmus test of confidence in a young band. Back in 2023, only Sam, on accordion and bouzouki, wore shades. A year later, seeing them for the third time, we were up to 40%, with Harkins taking the plunge. By autumn 2025, four out of five were wearing them.

### Then the record came out

When I first wrote about Brògeal, the debut album was still a few weeks away. _Tuesday Paper Club_ landed on 17 October 2025 via Play It Again Sam, produced by Richie Kennedy and recorded on the Isle of Lewis. Kennedy's speciality is bridging Celtic grit and modern punk-rock, and with Brògeal he got the balance right without polishing the soul out of them. The main challenge in that studio must have been bottling the untameable energy you feel when you see them live.

It went Top 10 in the Scottish album chart and reviews were positive: Far Out gave it 4.5, Clash an 8/10, with Stereogum naming them one of its best new artists of 2025.

The title track is the Brògeal I've known for years: a stompy, brash portrait of Scottish working-class life that Harkins first wrote as a poem behind the bar of an old man's boozer in Falkirk. There are shades of Ken Loach or _This Is England_, without being glib poverty-porn. It couldn't be, because, like Loach's or Meadows' work, it comes from the beating heart of the place it's describing.

People loved the Pogues, and still do, because beneath all the Guinness-stained back-slapping there was vulnerability. For every 'Sally MacLennane' there's 'A Pair of Brown Eyes'. If 'Tuesday Paper Club' is Brògeal's 'Sally MacLennane', then 'Go Home Tae Yer Bed' is their 'A Pair of Brown Eyes'. O'Callaghan wrote it about a dream encounter with his late father, and Josie Duncan's Gaelic interlude acts as an angelic compère, middle-manning the whole thing. It makes you want to give your dad a hug. It's still one of my most-played songs, and it put to bed my one worry about this band: the one-trick-pony problem.

### And now there's a Christmas song

Of course there is. This month they released 'The Last Noel', a duet with fellow Scot Rianne Downey about two people who haven't seen each other in years and don't know whether the other is still in love, or even still breathing. The band say it was inspired by the Pogues. 'Scottish Pogues' always felt like a slightly lazy shorthand, but when you release a boy-girl Christmas duet soaked in longing, you're inviting comparison.

What started as a 40-minute meet-cute at the Kentish Town Forum has become a three-year love affair: 8 shows and counting. Brògeal are proof that the best live bands don't always turn up with polish, PR or five pairs of sunglasses. They stumble in, covered in Guinness, and find a way to make chaos feel like Communion.

## What the support slot is actually for

The support slot isn't a warm-up. It's an audition in front of the people most likely to become your fans: people who've already paid for a night out to hear something in your neighbourhood of sound. That's priceless for a band. It's also brutal, because the room is half full, nobody knows your songs, and half the crowd is checking the set times.

Which is why the 40 people who actually stand at the front and listen matter so much. They're the ones who buy the £10 T-shirt, follow the band, drag a mate to the headline show at the Dublin Castle a year later, and a year after that tell everyone they saw them at the Forum when they were 'just the support'. That's how it works. Bands don't break; they get carried, one early arrival at a time.

So here's the challenge for December: get there early. Here are two bills where the support act is the reason to go.

## Two December gigs to get to early for

### 1. Scarsdale Fats (supporting Just Like Honey)

**Wed 2 December · The Troubadour, Earl's Court**

One of the most prolific live acts yet to release any music on DSPs, Scarsdale Fats are bubbling away nicely.

Musically they inhabit the space between '70s psych and '90s grunge: driving drums, spacey twin-lead guitars, big quiet-loud dynamics. They've already played the Lexington, the George Tavern, Dingwalls and Glastonbury. Headliners Just Like Honey are a dreamy grunge-pop duo from West London, and Mayfair and Hallowed Ground fill out the bill. The Troubadour is tiny and full of history, and it's on a Wednesday. Go.

I caught both of these artists on the same bill at Glastonbury’s Strummerville a few years ago, they’re both worth your time.

### 2. Heartworms (supporting Bloc Party & Interpol)

**Fri 4 & Sat 5 December · Olympia, Kensington**

Bloc Party and Interpol are closing their co-headline tour with two nights at Olympia, playing a lot of _Silent Alarm_ and _Antics_. In other words, two bands who came up through the 2004 indie toilet circuit are now playing an exhibition centre. The support is Heartworms, the moniker of Jojo Orme: dark, taut, militaristic post-punk that fits this bill perfectly. There's something nice about the next generation of moody post-punk opening for the last one. Twenty years from now, somebody will be writing this exact paragraph about whoever supports Heartworms.

## Encore: full circle at the Forum

One more for the diary. On Tuesday 15 December, the Pogues bring their Play The Hits tour to the O2 Forum Kentish Town, the room where Brògeal first ambushed me with a tin whistle and some tins of Guinness. The support is folk-pop veterans Martin Stephenson & the Daintees. It’s more watching the Vets XI than the Academy, but if you want to see where all this started, and who the next generation are borrowing from, there's no better room for it.

Whatever you go to before the year ends: skip the second pint. Get there early. The best night of your year might start at 7:30pm.
